figures.

|---|---:|

| Vorräte | 200 000 |
| Forderungen | 150 000 |
| liquide Mittel | 50 000 |
| Eigenkapital | 400 000 |
| langfristiges Fremdkapital | 350 000 |
| kurzfristiges Fremdkapital | 250 000 |

| Ratio | Value |
|---|---:|
| Bilanzsumme | 1 000 000 € |
| Eigenkapitalquote | 40 % |
| Anlagendeckung I | 66.7 % |
| Anlagendeckung II | 125 % |
| Liquidität I | 20 % |
| Liquidität II | 80 % |
| Liquidität III | 160 % |
| Working Capital | 150 000 € |

## Two ratios that say one thing

Working capital is positive exactly when the third degree of liquidity is
above 100 %. They are the same statement, once as an amount and once as a
ratio, and quoting both as separate evidence counts one fact twice.

## What the numbers say here

Coverage II above 100 % means the fixed assets are financed long term, which
is what the golden rule asks. The second degree of liquidity at 80 % is below
the usual 100 %, so short-term debt is not covered by cash and receivables
alone, and the company depends on selling inventory. The two findings sit in
the same balance sheet and point in different directions, which is normal.

## A ratio without a comparison

Thirty per cent equity is solid for a machine builder and unthinkable for a
bank. Alone, a ratio says nothing. It is compared with the previous year, with
the industry, and with what the loan agreement requires.
