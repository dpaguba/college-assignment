# Profitability and leverage

Return on sales times asset turnover times capital structure is the return on
equity. The decomposition is arithmetically trivial and that is the point: the
same figure comes out of three different companies.

| | Margin | Turnover | Leverage | Return on equity |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| high margin | 20 % | 0.5 | 2.0 | 20 % |
| high turnover | 4 % | 2.5 | 2.0 | 20 % |
| much debt | 5 % | 0.5 | 8.0 | 20 % |

Whoever compares only the last column holds the three for equal.

## Leverage in both directions

One million in assets, 250 000 € of equity, 750 000 € of debt at 5 %:

| Profit before interest | Return on assets | Return on equity |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 100 000 € | 10 % | **25 %** |
| 50 000 € | 5 % (rate 8 %) | **−4 %** |

The amplification is the same 3.0 in both rows, and it is the debt-to-equity
ratio. Leverage does not raise the return, it raises the spread. Checked
against the direct definition over two thousand random combinations of profit,
capital structure and interest rate.

## The contested denominator

Return on equity uses the equity that is **reported**, and reported equity is
lowered by every hidden reserve. A cautiously valued company shows a higher
return on equity than a fairly valued one with the same business. The ratio
rewards exactly the accounting policy it cannot see.
